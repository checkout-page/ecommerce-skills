# Service pricing

Set the number, the structure, and the presentation of a service price so it signals expertise and holds up in negotiation. Services are judged on quality before price, buyers overestimate how much time they'll use, and the digits of a quote change how the other side counters.

## When to use

- Choosing hourly vs flat/project fee vs retainer
- Writing a quote or proposal, especially one that will be negotiated
- Pricing packages, add-ons, and extras
- Deciding what to show on the pricing or about page (costs, team size, guarantees)

## When not to use

- Generic digit rules for products → `pricing`
- Wording a discount → `discounts`
- Finding the price level people will pay → `price-research`

## Inputs

Collect, or state your assumption:

- The service, typical scope, and expected hours or units
- Current hourly rate, if any; margin
- Who the buyer is: individual, business owner, or a professional buyer (procurement, purchasing manager)
- Whether the quote will be negotiated
- Whether the service is high-tech (software development, AI, engineering) or not
- How well-known the provider is

## Procedure

1. **Rounded by default.** Most services are bought on quality and trust, so the listed price should be rounded ($150/session, $2,000/month). Rounded prices feel right when the decision is about quality or feelings, and they make the choice easier. Just-below prices ($1,999) signal a low-price appeal; use them only for a promotion.
2. **Flat fee over hourly, priced above the hourly equivalent.** Quote a project or package fee that covers what most clients want ("logo, three concepts, unlimited revisions, final files") and charge more than the expected hourly total: a $6,000 fixed fee was preferred over $50/h for an expected 100 h ($5,000). Buyers overestimate their usage, dislike watching a meter run, and pay to cap the risk. Keep hourly only as a low-commitment entry for first-time clients.
3. **Precise numbers when the quote will be negotiated.** A precise opening quote ($3,450 rather than $3,500) anchors the counteroffer into smaller steps, so you get fewer but higher offers. With an **expert buyer** (procurement, purchasing managers) stop at moderate precision — $3,450, not $3,417.50 — or justify a highly precise figure with a cost breakdown; high precision without justification backfires with experts.
4. **Show your costs.** Show a per-project cost breakdown (your time, subcontractors, software, tools, overhead). The measured result — sales 22% higher, holding to a 55% margin — is from a physical product (a $115 wallet with a cost infographic); applying it to service proposals is a transfer, not a tested result. Group categories once there are more than ~8. This is the opposite of hiding the hourly math: show the structure, not the meter.
5. **Simple add-ons and components.** Rounded add-on prices with the same ending as the base ($2,000 project, $300 extra concept, not $287.50), one price per unit ($100 per extra page), no separate admin or service fee. Complex component pricing makes people assume you're the expensive option.
6. **Say you're small — unless the service is high-tech.** For low-tech services (design, coaching, writing, consulting) a small team reads as more motivated and higher quality; say so. For high-tech services, counter the size penalty by showing what you invest in R&D and tooling. A large low-tech firm should show employee passion instead.
7. **Extraordinary guarantee if you're unknown.** A guarantee far beyond the category norm (a year of free fixes, a full refund if the concept isn't approved) signals quality to buyers who know the service but not you.
8. **Discounts.** If you must discount, stay at or under 40% and apply the amount-vs-percent rule (project price ≥ 100 → amount off). Details in `discounts`.

## Output

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Structure | Flat project fee (hourly retained only as a trial option) |
| Quote | $3,450 — moderately precise, buyer is procurement |
| Included scope | 3 concepts, unlimited revisions, final files, usage rights |
| Add-ons | $300 per extra concept; $500 brand guide (rounded, same ending) |
| Shown on the proposal | Cost breakdown in ≤ 8 lines; "we're a two-person studio"; guarantee |
| Rejected | $3,400 (gives away nothing in negotiation), $3,399 (low-price signal), $3,417.50 (too precise for an expert buyer), hourly (meter effect, invites hour-cutting) |
| Why | one line per choice, citing the rule |

## Common mistakes

- Quoting a round $3,400 to a negotiating buyer "because it sounds confident" — a precise figure gets smaller, higher counteroffers.
- Using maximum precision ($3,417.50) with a procurement manager — moderate precision, or justify it with the breakdown.
- Hiding costs to protect margin — a cost breakdown raised sales, and the effect holds to 55% margin.
- Pricing the flat fee to match the hourly total — clients pay a premium to cap their spend.
- Bolting on a service fee or odd per-unit rates — simple components raise willingness to pay.
- Hiding that you're a small studio — for low-tech services it raises perceived quality.

## Quick reference

| Situation | Do |
|---|---|
| Listed price | Rounded |
| Hourly vs flat | Flat fee, above the hourly equivalent; hourly only as an entry option |
| Quote will be negotiated | Precise number |
| Expert buyer | Moderate precision, or justify with a breakdown |
| Trust page / proposal | Cost breakdown, ≤ 8 categories |
| Add-ons | Rounded, same ending, no service fee |
| Low-tech service | Say you're small |
| High-tech service | Show R&D investment |
| Unknown provider | Extraordinary guarantee |
| Discount | ≤ 40%, amount off when price ≥ 100 |
