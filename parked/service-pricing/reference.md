# Evidence: service-pricing

Peer-reviewed findings behind the `service-pricing` procedure. Effects are as reported in the studies, in the context they were measured. Most also appear in the generic `pricing`, `store-audit`, and `discounts` skills; they are repeated here so this skill stands alone.

## Rounded prices when quality or feelings drive the decision

**Rule.** For services, where the buyer is judging expertise rather than hunting for the lowest price, use rounded prices ($150, $2,000). Reserve just-below endings ($1,999) for promotions.
**Effect.** Rounded prices raised evaluations when the purchase was driven by feelings or quality; just-below endings were read as a low-price signal.
**Why.** People think and talk in round numbers, so a rounded price is processed fluently and "feels right"; a .99 ending cues bargain hunting.
**Source.** Wadhwa, M., & Zhang, K. (2015). This number just feels right: The impact of roundedness of price numbers on product evaluations. *Journal of Consumer Research*. https://doi.org/10.1086/678484 · Schindler, R. M. (2006). The 99 price ending as a signal of a low-price appeal. *Journal of Retailing*. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jretai.2005.11.001

## Flat fees beat hourly rates, even at a premium

**Rule.** Offer a flat project or package fee that covers what most clients want, priced above the expected hourly total. Keep hourly only as a low-commitment entry.
**Effect.** A $6,000 fixed price was more attractive than $50/h for an expected 100 h ($5,000). Purchasing professionals showed the same flat-rate preference.
**Why.** Buyers overestimate their usage, want certainty about the total, and dislike watching costs climb (the taximeter effect); a flat fee also removes the suspicion of padded hours.
**Source.** Kienzler, M., Kowalkowski, C., & Kindström, D. (2021). Purchasing professionals and the flat rate bias: Effects of price premiums, past usage, and relational ties on price plan choice. *Journal of Business Research*. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbusres.2021.04.024

## Precise opening quotes get higher counteroffers

**Rule.** When a quote will be negotiated down, open with a precise number ($3,450) rather than a round one ($3,500).
**Effect.** Precise asking prices led to fewer but higher offers; counteroffers moved in smaller steps.
**Why.** A precise figure looks calculated for a reason, so the other side negotiates in the same fine increments and treats the number as more legitimate.
**Source.** Backus, M., Blake, T., & Tadelis, S. (2019). On the empirical content of cheap-talk signaling: An application to bargaining. *Journal of Political Economy*. https://doi.org/10.1086/701699

## Too much precision backfires with expert buyers

**Rule.** With procurement or purchasing managers, use moderate precision ($3,450) or justify a highly precise figure ($3,417.50) with a cost breakdown.
**Effect.** Highly precise anchors reduced outcomes when the counterpart was an expert; moderate precision did not.
**Why.** Experts read unjustified precision as a tactic rather than a calculation.
**Source.** Loschelder, D. D., Friese, M., Schaerer, M., & Galinsky, A. D. (2016). The too-much-precision effect: When and why precise anchors backfire with experts. *Psychological Science*. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797616666074

## Show your costs

**Rule.** Break down what goes into the price (time, subcontractors, tools, overhead), by category; group once there are more than ~8 categories.
**Effect.** Sales were 22% higher for a $115 wallet shown with a cost infographic; the effect held with margins as high as 55%.
**Why.** Disclosing costs is read as honesty, which raises trust and liking; people already expect you to make a profit.
**Source.** Mohan, B., Buell, R. W., & John, L. K. (2020). Lifting the veil: The benefits of cost transparency. *Marketing Science*. https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.2019.1200

## Simple price components raise willingness to pay

**Rule.** Rounded add-on prices, the same ending across components, no separate service or admin fee, easy side-by-side comparison.
**Effect.** People were willing to pay more for simply structured prices than for cheaper but complex ones.
**Why.** Faced with complex prices, buyers skip the arithmetic and assume the offer is expensive; if they do calculate, they resent the effort and suspect it was deliberate.
**Source.** Homburg, C., Totzek, D., & Krämer, M. (2014). How price complexity takes its toll: The neglected role of a simplicity bias and fairness in price evaluations. *Journal of Business Research*. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbusres.2013.05.049

## Say you're small — unless the service is high-tech

**Rule.** Low-tech services: state that you are a small team. High-tech services: emphasize R&D and tooling. Large low-tech firms: show employee passion.
**Effect.** Products from small companies were judged higher quality, except for high-tech products, where larger companies were assumed to invest more in R&D.
**Why.** Buyers infer motivated, hands-on staff at small companies; for high-tech work they infer quality from resources.
**Source.** Woolley, K., Kupor, D., & Liu, P. J. (2022). Does company size shape product quality inferences? Larger companies make better high-tech products, but smaller companies make better low-tech products. *Journal of Marketing Research*. https://doi.org/10.1177/00222437221124857

## Extraordinary guarantees signal quality for unknown providers

**Rule.** Offer a guarantee far beyond the category norm when buyers know the service but not you.
**Effect.** A 10-year warranty on a hoodie made people 34.8% more likely to buy; strongest for buyers unfamiliar with the brand but familiar with the category.
**Why.** An unusually strong promise is a costly signal that only a confident provider would make.
**Source.** Study on extraordinary warranties as a quality signal for weakly familiar brands (2022). Overcoming the challenge of low familiarity: Can a weakly familiar brand signal quality with exceptionally strong warranty? *Journal of Business Research*. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbusres.2021.11.078

## Don't discount more than 40%

**Rule.** Above 40% reads as a quality warning; 60%+ tends to reduce sales, especially for unknown providers.
**Effect.** In daily-deal markets, deep discounts reduced sales.
**Why.** Price is read as a quality signal when the buyer cannot judge the work before purchase — which is the normal case for services.
**Source.** Cao, Z., Hui, K.-L., & Xu, H. (2018). When discounts hurt sales: The case of daily-deal markets. *Information Systems Research*. https://doi.org/10.1287/isre.2017.0772

## Amount off when the price is 100 or more

**Rule.** For a project fee of 100 or more, show the discount as an amount ("$300 off"), not a percentage.
**Effect.** For a 480-peso item, "120 pesos off" made people 16.3% more willing to buy than "25% off".
**Why.** People react to the size of the printed number.
**Source.** González, E. M., Esteva, E., Roggeveen, A. L., & Grewal, D. (2016). Amount off versus percentage off — when does it matter? *Journal of Business Research*. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbusres.2015.08.014
