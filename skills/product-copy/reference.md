# Evidence: product-copy

Peer-reviewed findings behind the `product-copy` procedure. Effects are as reported in the studies, in the context they were measured.

## Three claims, not four

**Rule.** Make three positive claims about a product or brand, not more.
**Effect.** Three claims were 10.4% more persuasive than four.
**Why.** Three is the smallest number in which people perceive a pattern and feel ready to decide. When people know a message is trying to persuade them, skepticism kicks in from the fourth claim.
**Source.** Shu, S. B., & Carlson, K. A. (2014). When three charms but four alarms: Identifying the optimal number of claims in persuasion settings. *Journal of Marketing*. https://doi.org/10.1509/jm.11.0504

## Don't dilute your best features with merely good ones

**Rule.** Mention only your strongest features. Adding good-but-not-great features alongside them lowers the overall impression.
**Effect.** Mixed lists were judged at the level of the weakest item.
**Why.** People average rather than sum the quality of claims.
**Source.** Weaver, K., Garcia, S. M., & Schwarz, N. (2012). The presenter's paradox. *Journal of Consumer Research*. https://doi.org/10.1086/664497

## Rational messages up, emotional messages down

**Rule.** In a vertical layout, place functional or rational messages in the upper part and emotional messages in the lower part.
**Effect.** Messages placed according to the metaphor were more effective.
**Why.** People hold a "head is up, heart is down" spatial metaphor; matching placement to message type makes it feel right.
**Source.** Cian, L., Krishna, A., & Schwarz, N. (2015). Positioning rationality and emotion: Rationality is up and emotion is down. *Journal of Consumer Research*. https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucv046

## Plain language

**Rule.** Use words the target audience already understands. Define or drop technical terms.
**Effect.** Hard-to-understand terms made a product seem worse and significantly more expensive.
**Why.** Processing difficulty is misread as a property of the product.
**Source.** No primary citation on file; treat as a practitioner tip.

## Evocative color names

**Rule.** Name colors with unusual, emotional names ("midday sky blue") rather than generic ones ("light blue").
**Effect.** Products with unusual names were liked more and chosen more often.
**Why.** An unexpected name prompts the reader to work out why it fits, and the resolved puzzle feels rewarding.
**Source.** Miller, E. G., & Kahn, B. E. (2005). Shades of meaning: The effect of color and flavor names on consumer choice. *Journal of Consumer Research*. https://doi.org/10.1086/429602

## Quantities in intuitive units (uses, pieces) rather than standardized measures

**Rule.** Say "20 washes" not "200 g of detergent", "8 cans" not "96 oz".
**Effect.** Units people intuitively understand raised product evaluations and willingness to pay in the cited studies.
**Why.** Uses and pieces map directly onto the buyer's experience; grams and ounces require conversion.
**Source.** Lembregts, C., & Van den Bergh, B. (2019). Making each unit count: The role of discretizing units in quantity expressions. *Journal of Consumer Research*. https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucy036 — and Lembregts, C., & Pandelaere, M. (2013). Are all units created equal? The effect of default units on product evaluations. *Journal of Consumer Research*. https://doi.org/10.1086/668533.

## Smaller units read as more precise and credible

**Rule.** Express precision claims in the smaller unit: 180 minutes rather than 3 hours; 32 oz rather than a quarter gallon. Strongest for time.
**Effect.** Claims in smaller units were judged more precise and more believable.
**Why.** A larger numeral in a finer unit implies the maker measured carefully.
**Source.** Zhang, Y. C., & Schwarz, N. (2011). How and why 1 year differs from 365 days: A conversational logic analysis of inferences from the granularity of quantitative expressions. *Journal of Consumer Research*. https://doi.org/10.1086/662612

## Feature branded ingredients and components

**Rule.** Name a branded component prominently (Nutella-filled cake, Gorilla Glass screen).
**Effect.** Demand increased up to 40% in experiments.
**Why.** The component brand's reputation transfers to the host product.
**Source.** No primary citation on file; treat as a practitioner tip.

## Science-based messaging only for utilitarian products

**Rule.** For products bought for pleasure (cookies, perfume, treats), avoid "lab-designed", "scientifically formulated" and similar. For products bought for effectiveness, science-based language helps.
**Effect.** Hedonic products with science-based descriptions were chosen up to 30% less often.
**Why.** Science implies effort, control, and lack of warmth, which clashes with indulgence but fits performance.
**Source.** No primary citation on file; treat as a practitioner tip.

## Describe how the product feels to the touch

**Rule.** When feel matters (clothing, textiles, skincare), describe the texture explicitly.
**Effect.** Online clothing shoppers name the inability to feel materials as a key pain point; tactile descriptions reduce it.
**Why.** Verbal haptic description partially substitutes for physical touch and lowers purchase uncertainty.
**Source.** Study on tactile product descriptions in online apparel retail. *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services* (2021). https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jretconser.2020.102378

## Frame the product as an experience to attract reviews

**Rule.** Describe the social or emotional experience around the product (people drinking coffee together) rather than its features (bean origin).
**Effect.** Similar Amazon products framed as experiences had more reviews.
**Why.** Experiences are more shareable and more worth talking about than specifications.
**Source.** No primary citation on file; treat as a practitioner tip.
