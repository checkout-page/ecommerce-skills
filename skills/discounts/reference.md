# Evidence: discounts

Peer-reviewed findings behind the `discounts` procedure. Effects are as reported in the studies, in the context they were measured.

## Amount off when the full price is 100 or more; percentage off below 100

**Rule.** Show whichever numeral is bigger. Above 100, the amount is the bigger number; below 100, the percentage is.
**Effect.** For a 480-peso jacket, "120 pesos off" made people 16.3% more willing to buy than "25% off". For a 48-peso item the percentage framing won.
**Why.** People react to the size of the number in the discount and rarely do the arithmetic to compare.
**Source.** González, E. M., Esteva, E., Roggeveen, A. L., & Grewal, D. (2016). Amount off versus percentage off — when does it matter? *Journal of Business Research*. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbusres.2015.08.014

## State a percentage discount against the sale price ("was 25% higher")

**Rule.** Compute the percentage on the discounted price, not the original. A $2 discount on $10 is "now 20% lower" or "was 25% higher" ($2/$8); the second is a bigger number. Works for price increases and feature claims too ("40% longer battery").
**Effect.** Larger numerals raised purchase likelihood across the studies' experiments.
**Why.** People weight the number more than the words around it.
**Source.** Guha, A., Biswas, A., Grewal, D., Verma, S., Banerjee, S., & Nordfält, J. (2018). Reframing the discount as a comparison against the sale price: Does it make the discount more attractive? *Journal of Marketing Research*. https://doi.org/10.1509/jmr.16.0599

## The "was X% higher" framing stops working past 100%

**Rule.** If the recalculated percentage exceeds 100%, people drop the "100" and read only the remainder (150% reads as 50%). Use plain "% off" or the amount instead.
**Effect.** A $12 discount on a $20 item (150% higher than the $8 sale price) was perceived as roughly 50% higher.
**Why.** Percentages over 100 are processed as "100 plus something", and the "something" dominates.
**Source.** Study on consumer interpretation of percentages above 100. *Journal of Consumer Research* (2022). https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucac006

## Store-wide coupons in percent; product-specific coupons in amounts

**Rule.** "Everything 15% off" for a store-wide promotion; "$2 off brand X" for a product or brand promotion.
**Effect.** A store-wide percentage coupon raised sales 42.3% in one experiment.
**Why.** A percentage with no ceiling puts people in a "the more I spend, the more I save" mode. A fixed amount on one product makes the foregone saving concrete and needs no arithmetic. Watch for hoarding: send store-wide coupons to segments that will buy new items.
**Source.** Montazeri, S., Tamaddoni, A., Stakhovych, S., & Ewing, M. (2021). Empirical decomposition of customer responses to discount coupons in online FMCG retailing. *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services*. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jretconser.2020.102340

## Keep the sale price ending the same as the full price

**Rule.** $26.75 → $16.75, not $16.99. Always show both prices side by side.
**Effect.** Consistent endings increased perceived cheapness and purchase likelihood.
**Why.** The subtraction becomes trivial, so people actually work out and appreciate the saving.
**Source.** Hung, H.-H., Cheng, Y.-H., Chuang, S.-C., Yu, A. P.-I., & Lin, Y.-T. (2021). Consistent price endings increase consumers' perceptions of cheapness. *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services*. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jretconser.2021.102590

## Don't discount more than 40%

**Rule.** Above 40% is a quality warning; 60%+ tends to reduce sales, especially for unknown brands or hard-to-judge products. Clearance with a stated reason is the exception.
**Effect.** In daily-deal markets, deep discounts reduced sales. A related experiment found people who drank an energy drink bought at a discount solved fewer puzzles — the low price produced a negative placebo effect.
**Why.** Price is read as a quality signal when other signals are weak.
**Source.** Cao, Z., Hui, K.-L., & Xu, H. (2018). When discounts hurt sales: The case of daily-deal markets. *Information Systems Research*. https://doi.org/10.1287/isre.2017.0772 — placebo experiment: Shiv, B., Carmon, Z., & Ariely, D. (2005). Placebo effects of marketing actions: Consumers may get what they pay for. *Journal of Marketing Research*. https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.2005.42.4.383

## Time limits work online mainly for awareness, not immediate sales

**Rule.** Use short windows (24–48 h) with a stated reason and a timezone. Don't expect the deadline alone to convert.
**Effect.** Online, limited-time promotions raised engagement (opens, visits) but were weak at driving immediate purchases, unlike in physical stores.
**Why.** Offline scarcity reads as value; online it reads as manufactured urgency, which distracts from the deal.
**Source.** Hmurovic, J., Lamberton, C., & Goldsmith, K. (2022). Examining the efficacy of time scarcity marketing promotions in online retail. *Journal of Marketing Research*. https://doi.org/10.1177/00222437221118856

## Limit by time, never by quantity

**Rule.** "Ends Sunday" is fine. "Only 500 units" is not.
**Effect.** People who missed out on a quantity-limited offer reported anger toward the brand and intentions to switch to competitors. Time-limited offers did not produce this.
**Why.** Missing a unit cap feels like being beaten by others; missing a deadline feels like one's own timing.
**Source.** Biraglia, A., Usrey, B., & Ulqinaku, A. (2021). The downside of scarcity: Scarcity appeals can trigger consumer anger and brand switching intentions. *Psychology & Marketing*. https://doi.org/10.1002/mar.21489

## Put the condition before the reward

**Rule.** "Spend $100 and get $20 off", not "Get $20 off by spending $100".
**Effect.** The promotion reads as a reward rather than a restriction.
**Why.** Order of presentation sets the frame: leading with the threshold makes the discount feel earned.
**Source.** No primary citation on file; treat as a practitioner tip.

## Red prices look cheaper to men

**Rule.** If the audience is mostly men, show every price in red. Never mix red and black prices.
**Effect.** Men rated red prices as if they were far lower than the same prices in black; women were unaffected, and the effect disappears when the purchase is scrutinized closely.
**Why.** Red draws male attention and is processed less analytically; women evaluate prices more thoroughly regardless of color. Mixed coloring flags the red ones as manipulation.
**Source.** Puccinelli, N. M., Chandrashekaran, R., Grewal, D., & Suri, R. (2013). Are men seduced by red? The effect of red versus black prices on price perceptions. *Journal of Retailing*. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jretai.2013.01.002 — mixed-color backfire: Red price? Red flag! Eye-tracking reveals how one red price can hurt a retailer (2020). *Psychology & Marketing*. https://doi.org/10.1002/mar.21331
