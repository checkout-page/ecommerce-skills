# Evidence: promotions

Peer-reviewed findings behind the `promotions` procedure. Effects are as reported in the studies, in the context they were measured.

## Gifts beat discounts for pre-orders of distant launches

**Rule.** Launch 3+ weeks out → offer a gift (complementary product, extra content). Within about a week → gift or discount. Short windows for utilitarian products; hedonic products tolerate long ones.
**Effect.** For a Halloween-themed pastry, an extra free pastry produced far more pre-orders than a $25 discount when the launch was 21 days away; at 3 days the two were closer.
**Why.** A gift builds pleasant anticipation; a discount is a rational, deferred saving, and people discount future rewards.
**Source.** Mukherjee, A., Smith, R., & Burton, S. (2021). The effect of positive anticipatory utility on product pre-order evaluations and choices. *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science*. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11747-021-00810-1

## Frame the product people want as the free gift in a bundle

**Rule.** "Buy the weighing scale, get the fitness tracker free", targeted at people looking for the tracker. Comparable values only.
**Effect.** Sales were up to 78% higher when the target product was framed as the gift rather than the purchased item.
**Why.** Finding the thing you were already looking for offered as a gift feels lucky.
**Source.** Liu, M. W., Wei, C., Yang, L., & Keh, H. T. (2022). Feeling lucky: How framing the target product as a free gift enhances purchase intention. *International Journal of Research in Marketing*. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijresmar.2021.07.001

## "Buy X, get Y free" cuts returns versus "X and Y for a special price"

**Rule.** Frame bundle promotions as a free gift, not a bundle price.
**Effect.** Product returns fell by up to 50%.
**Why.** A gift isn't evaluated against a price paid, so it's less likely to be sent back.
**Source.** Study on gift vs bundle promotion framing and product returns (2018). "Retail is detail! Give consumers a gift rather than a bundle": Promotion framing and consumer product returns. *Psychology & Marketing*. https://doi.org/10.1002/mar.21154

## "For $0" converts better than "free"

**Rule.** Replace "free" with "for $0". The study tested the "$0" numeral specifically; "at no cost" is an untested paraphrase.
**Effect.** Higher conversion for the $0 framing across the reported studies.
**Why.** "$0" keeps money in mind and triggers loss aversion (money not lost); "free" frames a gain and strips the item of value.
**Source.** Koo, J., & Suk, K. (2020). Is $0 better than free? Consumer response to "$0" versus "free" framing of a free promotion. *Journal of Retailing*. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jretai.2019.11.006

## Charge a token amount for upgrades

**Rule.** Offer the larger size or add-on for a nominal fee (1¢, ¥0.1) rather than free.
**Effect.** A camera deal with a memory-card upgrade was rated 39% better at ¥0.1 than free; 17.1% more people took the larger juice can at ¥0.1 than free.
**Why.** "Free" gives no reference point, so people judge the deal on the main price. A token fee gives a number to compare against the upgrade's benefit, and it wins.
**Source.** Mao, W. (2016). Sometimes "fee" is better than "free": Token promotional pricing and consumer reactions to price promotion offering product upgrades. *Journal of Retailing*. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jretai.2015.09.001

## Invented, relevant special days lift promotions

**Rule.** Theme the promotion around a creative and fitting day; generic sales and obvious holidays don't get the lift.
**Effect.** People were 25% more likely to buy from a picnic retailer when a one-day sale was framed as "National Picnic Day Sale" rather than "Annual One Day Sale".
**Why.** Unexpected, appropriate creativity improves feelings toward the brand and the enjoyment of the purchase.
**Source.** Zane, D. M., Reczek, R. W., & Haws, K. L. (2021). Promoting Pi Day: Consumer responses to special day-themed promotions. *Journal of Consumer Psychology*. https://doi.org/10.1002/jcpy.1271

## Deadlines, not unit caps

**Rule.** Limit by time, never by quantity.
**Effect.** People who missed a quantity-limited offer reported anger toward the brand and intention to switch; time-limited offers didn't cause this.
**Why.** Missing a unit cap feels like losing to others; missing a deadline feels like one's own choice.
**Source.** Biraglia, A., Usrey, B., & Ulqinaku, A. (2021). The downside of scarcity: Scarcity appeals can trigger consumer anger and brand switching intentions. *Psychology & Marketing*. https://doi.org/10.1002/mar.21489

## Online deadlines drive engagement more than sales

**Rule.** Use short (24–48 h), justified deadlines with a timezone, and don't rely on them alone.
**Effect.** Limited-time promotions online raised opens and visits but were weak at driving immediate purchase, unlike in stores.
**Why.** Online, time scarcity reads as manufactured urgency and distracts from the offer's value.
**Source.** Hmurovic, J., Lamberton, C., & Goldsmith, K. (2022). Examining the efficacy of time scarcity marketing promotions in online retail. *Journal of Marketing Research*. https://doi.org/10.1177/00222437221118856

## Keep 10–30% of sold-out options visible

**Rule.** Show sold-out variants marked as sold out; if more than 30% are sold out, hide some or restock.
**Effect.** Sales increased up to 31% among undecided buyers when sold-out options stayed visible; the effect reversed at high proportions.
**Why.** A sold-out option signals that others wanted it, raising quality perceptions of the siblings; too many sold out and people feel their choice is constrained and defer.
**Source.** Tian, J., Chen, R., & Xu, X. (2022). A good way to boost sales? Effects of the proportion of sold-out options on purchase behavior. *International Journal of Research in Marketing*. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijresmar.2021.04.002 · Phantom options: *Basic and Applied Social Psychology* (1992). https://doi.org/10.1207/s15324834basp1301_9

## Pick-your-price works; pay-what-you-want doesn't

**Rule.** Offer 3–4 fixed price options; expect most to choose the lowest. Don't use for basket purchases. Never let people choose any amount.
**Effect.** Pick-your-price raised purchase likelihood; pay-what-you-want has repeatedly reduced sales.
**Why.** A short menu gives a feeling of control at low effort; an open field is so effortful that people walk away.
**Source.** Wang, C. X., Beck, J., & Yuan, H. (2021). The control–effort trade-off in participative pricing: How easing pricing decisions enhances purchase outcomes. *Journal of Marketing*. https://doi.org/10.1177/0022242921990351 · Pay-what-you-want: *Journal of Business Research* (2022). https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbusres.2022.02.014

## Uncertain rewards motivate more than fixed ones

**Rule.** Make loyalty or promotional rewards variable ("20% or 80% off your 11th order").
**Effect.** Fewer people opt in initially, but those who do repeat the rewarded behavior often enough to more than offset the drop.
**Why.** Uncertainty about the size of a reward makes pursuing it more engaging.
**Source.** Shen, L., Fishbach, A., & Hsee, C. K. (2015). The motivating-uncertainty effect: Uncertainty increases resource investment in the process of reward pursuit. *Journal of Consumer Research*. https://doi.org/10.1086/679418
