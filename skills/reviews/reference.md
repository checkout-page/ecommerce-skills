# Evidence: reviews

Peer-reviewed findings behind the `reviews` procedure. Effects are as reported in the studies, in the context they were measured.

## Ask for reviews 9–13 days after purchase, not the next day

**Rule.** Send review requests 9–13 days after the purchase or experience for most products; sooner for products judged instantly, later for those that take time to evaluate.
**Effect.** Two field experiments: reminders sent 24 hours after returning from a holiday reduced the number of reviews. The negative effect was stronger for younger people.
**Why.** A reminder while the experience is still fresh feels like pressure and triggers reactance — people push back when they feel their autonomy is threatened. Later, the reminder jogs memory without the pushback.
**Source.** Jung, M., Ryu, S., Han, S. P., & Cho, D. (2023). Ask for reviews at the right time: Evidence from two field experiments. *Journal of Marketing*. https://doi.org/10.1177/00222429221143329

## Open feedback requests with a positive open-ended question

**Rule.** Start with "What did you enjoy most about [product]?" as free text, then continue with standard questions.
**Effect.** Customers who responded to a positively framed open-ended feedback request spent 131% more afterward than those who didn't. Merely receiving the request had a positive effect.
**Why.** People form a better impression of a company that asks their opinion, and recalling and articulating the best parts strengthens positive memories of the product.
**Source.** Bone, S. A., Lemon, K. N., Voorhees, C. M., Liljenquist, K. A., Fombelle, P. W., DeTienne, K. B., & Money, R. B. (2017). "Mere measurement plus": How solicitation of open-ended positive feedback influences customer purchase behavior. *Journal of Marketing Research*. https://doi.org/10.1509/jmr.14.0232

## Score-gating is not supported here and is legally risky

**Rule.** Do not route low scorers away from the public review path while sending high scorers to it.
**Effect.** None of the studies behind this skill tested or supports gating. Regulators including the US FTC (2024 rule on fake and suppressed reviews) and the UK CMA treat selective solicitation that suppresses negative reviews as deceptive.
**Why.** Included as a guardrail, not a finding.
**Source.** No primary citation on file; regulatory guidance, not a study.

## The first review shapes the ones that follow

**Rule.** At launch, ask all early customers for a review promptly, through the same path for everyone; don't leave the first review to chance by waiting. Do not select who gets asked by how happy they are.
**Effect.** On Amazon and Best Buy, products whose first review was negative had an average rating 0.29 stars lower one year later than expected.
**Why.** Early reviews anchor later reviewers.
**Source.** Park, S., Shin, W., & Xie, J. (2021). The fateful first consumer review. *Marketing Science*. https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.2020.1264

## A 4.0–4.5 average sells more than 4.5–5.0

**Rule.** Don't chase a perfect score.
**Effect.** Products with average ratings between 4 and 4.5 stars sold more than those between 4.5 and 5.
**Why.** Perfect ratings look suspicious.
**Source.** No primary citation on file; treat as a practitioner tip.

## Irrelevant negative reviews help

**Rule.** Leave negative reviews whose complaint doesn't apply to most buyers; reply to them instead of removing them.
**Effect.** People rated an espresso machine 16% better when, alongside four 5-star reviews, it had a 1-star review about an irrelevant issue (water type affecting taste).
**Why.** An irrelevant negative makes the positive reviews look authentic.
**Source.** No primary citation on file; treat as a practitioner tip.

## Reply to every review

**Rule.** Respond to positive and negative reviews alike.
**Effect.** On TripAdvisor and Expedia, when businesses started responding, star ratings rose by 0.12 on average and they received 12% more reviews.
**Why.** Visible responsiveness raises the perceived care and lowers the cost of reviewing.
**Source.** Proserpio, D., & Zervas, G. (2017). Online reputation management: Estimating the impact of management responses on consumer reviews. *Marketing Science*. https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.2017.1043

## Reply to public complaints once, fast, with empathy, then move it private

**Rule.** One prompt reply showing empathy and an explanation, then an invitation to continue privately. Use a separate support account if complaint volume is high.
**Effect.** Replying reduces the negative impact of the complaint but increases its visibility on platforms that boost replied-to posts; a single well-judged reply captures the benefit while limiting the exposure.
**Why.** The reply is read by everyone, not just the complainer; repeated public exchanges amplify the complaint.
**Source.** Golmohammadi, A., Havakhor, T., Gauri, D. K., & Comprix, J. (2021). Complaint publicization in social media. *Journal of Marketing*. https://doi.org/10.1177/00222429211002183

## Answer rude complaints with humor about the situation

**Rule.** For rude public complaints, reply with humor aimed at the situation, not the person. It must actually be funny.
**Effect.** Onlookers engaged more with the humorous reply and were 18.3% more likely to buy than when there was no response.
**Why.** Humor defuses the complaint for the audience and turns the exchange into positive visibility.
**Source.** Study on observers' reactions to humorous complaint responses (2021). How do observers react to companies' humorous responses to online public complaints? *Journal of Service Research*. https://doi.org/10.1177/1094670521989448

## Frame the product as an experience to earn more reviews

**Rule.** Describe the emotional or social experience around the product rather than its features.
**Effect.** On Amazon, similar products framed as experiences had more reviews.
**Why.** Experiences are more natural to talk about than specifications.
**Source.** No primary citation on file; treat as a practitioner tip.
