# Evidence: product-page

Peer-reviewed findings behind the `product-page` audit. Effects are as reported in the studies, in the context they were measured. Copy and visual rules are summarized here; their full evidence lives in the `product-copy` and `product-visuals` skills.

## Show a view count or a purchase count; switch to purchases as numbers grow

**Rule.** Display how many people viewed or bought the product. Start with views; switch to purchases when counts get large; show both if the views-to-purchase rate is 5% or more. Test the timeframe (today, this week, 90 days).
**Effect.** Purchase intentions increased up to about 58% in experiments.
**Why.** Views and purchases signal popularity and quality. Very large view counts (e.g. 15,600) are hard to process mentally and lose force, so purchase counts work better at scale.
**Source.** Das, G., Spence, M. T., & Agarwal, J. (2021). Social selling cues: The dynamics of posting numbers viewed and bought on customers' purchase intentions. *International Journal of Research in Marketing*. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijresmar.2021.01.001

## Keep sold-out options visible, but no more than 10–30% of options

**Rule.** Mark sold-out variants as sold out instead of hiding them. If more than 30% of options are sold out, hide some or restock.
**Effect.** Sales increased up to 31% among shoppers who hadn't settled on a specific option.
**Why.** A sold-out option signals that enough people wanted it to exhaust stock, and that quality inference spills onto the remaining options. Too many sold-out options make people feel their choice is constrained, and they defer or leave.
**Source.** Tian, J., Chen, R., & Xu, X. (2022). A good way to boost sales? Effects of the proportion of sold-out options on purchase behavior. *International Journal of Research in Marketing*. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijresmar.2021.04.002

## Use quantity CTAs for products bought in multiple units

**Rule.** Replace a single "Add to cart" with buttons for quantities (Buy 1 / Buy 2 / Buy 5). Start with three and test.
**Effect.** In 37 experiments, conversions rose 14% on average and sales up to 28%.
**Why.** People split buying into "should I buy?" and "how many?". Framing the CTA around the second question moves them past the first.
**Source.** Duke, K., & Amir, O. (2023). The importance of selling formats: When integrating purchase and quantity decisions increases sales. *Marketing Science*. https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.2022.1364

## Partition choices into steps of 2–5 similar options

**Rule.** With more than a handful of options, present them one group at a time (technical first, then aesthetic; larger decisions before smaller). Let people go back and change earlier steps.
**Effect.** Partitioned choice reduced choice overload and improved satisfaction with the chosen option.
**Why.** Different attributes call on different criteria (price, aesthetics, practicality); evaluating them all at once overloads the decision.
**Source.** Dorn, M., Messner, C., & Wänke, M. (2016). Partitioning the choice task makes Starbucks coffee taste better. *Journal of Marketing Behavior*. https://doi.org/10.1561/107.00000023

## Keep add-on and upgrade prices simple

**Rule.** Round add-on prices ($0.10/minute, not $0.0831/minute), keep the same last digit across base and add-on prices ($22 and $32), and prefer flat rates.
**Effect.** 68% of people in one study were willing to pay more for a simpler but more expensive option; complex prices were judged more expensive and less fair.
**Why.** People skip the arithmetic on complex prices and assume you're expensive, and resent the effort if they do calculate.
**Source.** Homburg, C., Totzek, D., & Krämer, M. (2013). How price complexity takes its toll: The neglected role of a simplicity bias and fairness in price evaluations. *Journal of Business Research*. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbusres.2013.05.049. Flat-rate preference: Kienzler, M., Kowalkowski, C., & Kindström, D. (2021). Purchasing professionals and the flat rate bias. *Journal of Business Research*. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbusres.2021.04.024

## Show the price below the product

**Rule.** Position the price underneath the product image, not above it.
**Effect.** The same $2.49 price was judged 9% lower when placed below the product. In a physical liquor store, sales were 35.2% higher with prices below the bottles.
**Why.** "Down = less, up = more" is a deeply held spatial metaphor; a lower position makes the price feel smaller.
**Source.** Barone, M., Coulter, K., & Li, X. (2020). The upside of down: Presenting a price in a low or high location influences how consumers evaluate it. *Journal of Retailing*. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jretai.2020.02.003

## Three claims, not four

**Rule.** Make three positive claims about the product, not more.
**Effect.** Three claims were 10.4% more persuasive than four.
**Why.** Three is enough to see a pattern; from the fourth claim on, people who know they're being persuaded become skeptical.
**Source.** Shu, S. B., & Carlson, K. A. (2014). When three charms but four alarms: Identifying the optimal number of claims in persuasion settings. *Journal of Marketing*. https://doi.org/10.1509/jm.11.0504

## Say you're a small business (unless the product is high-tech)

**Rule.** Small companies should say so. High-tech small companies should instead emphasize R&D spend; large low-tech companies should show employee passion.
**Effect.** Products from small companies were judged higher quality, except high-tech products, where larger companies were assumed to make better products.
**Why.** People infer employee motivation from company size for ordinary products, and R&D resources from size for technical ones.
**Source.** Woolley, K., Kupor, D., & Liu, P. J. (2022). Does company size shape product quality inferences? Larger companies make better high-tech products, but smaller companies make better low-tech products. *Journal of Marketing Research*. https://doi.org/10.1177/00222437221124857

## Offer an extraordinary warranty

**Rule.** An unusually long warranty (10 years on a hoodie) signals quality, most of all to people who know the category but not your brand.
**Effect.** People were 34.8% more likely to buy a hoodie with a 10-year warranty.
**Why.** A long warranty is a costly signal that the maker expects the product to last.
**Source.** Study on extraordinary warranties as a quality signal for weakly familiar brands (2022). Overcoming the challenge of low familiarity: Can a weakly familiar brand signal quality with exceptionally strong warranty? *Journal of Business Research*. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbusres.2021.11.078

## Condensed copy and visual rules

The audit's copy checks (best features only, intuitive units, plain language, science-based wording only for utilitarian products) and visual checks (video for hedonic products, gaze away, first-person hand, shine, spacing, right-side placement, complementary items) are summarized from the `product-copy` and `product-visuals` skills; see those reference files for sources and effect sizes. Two of the copy checks — plain language and science-based wording only for utilitarian products — have no primary citation on file and are practitioner tips.
