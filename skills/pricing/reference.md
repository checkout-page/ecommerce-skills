# Evidence: pricing

Peer-reviewed findings behind the `pricing` procedure. Effects are as reported in the studies, in the context they were measured.

## Rounded prices when the decision is about quality or feelings

**Rule.** Rounded prices ($80, $200) are the default for premium, risky, service, and hedonic purchases, and whenever you want the choice to feel effortless.
**Effect.** Rounded prices raised evaluations of hedonic purchases; non-rounded prices did better for utilitarian ones, because each fits the way the buyer is thinking.
**Why.** People think and talk in round numbers, so rounded prices are processed fluently and "feel right" when the buyer is going on feeling rather than calculation.
**Source.** Wadhwa, M., & Zhang, K. (2015). This number just feels right: The impact of roundedness of price numbers on product evaluations. *Journal of Consumer Research*. https://doi.org/10.1086/678484

## Rounded prices make choices easier

**Rule.** When you want to reduce effort for the customer (better UX, faster decisions), round.
**Effect.** Round prices lowered the effort of evaluating an offer and raised purchase likelihood in the conditions studied.
**Why.** Fewer digits to process; the number maps onto how people already think about money.
**Source.** Study in *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science* (2015). https://doi.org/10.1007/s11747-015-0428-7

## Rounded asking prices when bids will go up

**Rule.** If demand is strong and the price is negotiable upward (hot housing market, auction), open with a round number.
**Effect.** Round list prices in a tight market attracted higher final prices than precise ones, because bidders moved in larger increments.
**Why.** A round anchor sets a coarse mental unit ($1,000 steps); a precise one sets a fine unit.
**Source.** Study in *Management Science* (2021). https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2019.3570

## Just-below prices work through the left digit

**Rule.** $2.99 vs $3.00 works; $2.95 vs $2.99 doesn't. The leftmost digit has to fall.
**Effect.** Perceived price differences were driven by the left digit far more than by the actual difference.
**Why.** People encode the first digit and discount the rest.
**Source.** Thomas, M., & Morwitz, V. (2005). Penny wise and pound foolish: The left-digit effect in price cognition. *Journal of Consumer Research*. https://doi.org/10.1086/429600 · Manning, K. C., & Sprott, D. E. (2009). Price endings, left-digit effects, and choice. *Journal of Consumer Research*. https://doi.org/10.1086/597215

## Small left digits make a discount feel bigger

**Rule.** A promotion price that starts with 1 or 4 feels like a bigger cut than one starting with 7.
**Effect.** Perceived discount size varied with the magnitude of the left digit, holding the actual discount constant.
**Why.** Left-digit anchoring again: the smaller the first digit, the cheaper the whole number feels.
**Source.** Study in *Marketing Letters* (2015). https://doi.org/10.1007/s11002-015-9387-5

## Just-below endings signal "low price", not "high quality"

**Rule.** Use .99 endings when price is the pitch; avoid them when quality is.
**Effect.** 99-endings were read as a signal of a low-price appeal, and a weaker quality signal.
**Why.** Customers have learned the convention: 99-endings mean discount retailing.
**Source.** Schindler, R. M. (2006). The 99 price ending as a signal of a low-price appeal. *Journal of Retailing*. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jretai.2005.11.001

## Four-figure prices: repeat a low digit instead of 999

**Rule.** ¥2,111 beats ¥1,999. In currencies where everyday prices have four or more digits, repeating a digit below 5 (ideally 1) in the last three places gives a stronger value feeling than a just-below ending.
**Effect.** The repeated low digits overrode the just-below effect in the reported experiments.
**Why.** Repetition of a low digit makes the whole number feel small; at four figures the left digit is less salient.
**Source.** No primary citation on file; treat as a practitioner tip.

## 8 is the lucky ending in Japan and China

**Rule.** Where 8 carries a meaning of luck or prosperity, use an 8 ending rather than 9.
**Effect.** The cited studies document that 8-endings dominate Japanese and Chinese price advertising — a prevalence finding, not a measured sales lift.
**Why.** Cultural number symbolism overrides the western 99 convention.
**Source.** Study in *International Marketing Review* (2009). https://doi.org/10.1108/02651330910933186 · Study in *Journal of International Marketing* (2003). https://doi.org/10.1509/jimk.11.2.101.20161

## Keep tiers on the same left digit if you want upgrades

**Rule.** $40.50 → $48.50 for the upgrade, not $39.99 → $48.50.
**Effect.** Crossing a left-digit boundary made the upgrade feel like a larger jump and reduced upgrade rates.
**Why.** The perceived gap between tiers is driven by the left digits, not the true difference.
**Source.** Study in *Journal of Consumer Research* (2021). https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucab049

## Precise opening prices get higher counteroffers

**Rule.** When the buyer will negotiate down (used cars, real estate, B2B), open with a precise figure ($2,865, not $3,000).
**Effect.** Precise asking prices produced fewer offers but higher ones; counteroffers moved in smaller steps.
**Why.** A precise number implies it was calculated for a reason, so it feels legitimate and sets a fine negotiation unit.
**Source.** Backus, M., Blake, T., & Tadelis, S. (2019). On the empirical content of cheap-talk signaling: An application to bargaining. *Journal of Political Economy*. https://doi.org/10.1086/701699

## Too much precision backfires with experts

**Rule.** With expert buyers (purchasing managers, surveyors), use moderate precision ($23,750) or justify a very precise number with a breakdown.
**Effect.** Very precise anchors ($23,721.85) lowered experts' evaluations and offers relative to moderately precise ones; novices reacted the opposite way.
**Why.** Experts read excessive precision as false confidence.
**Source.** Loschelder, D. D., Friese, M., Schaerer, M., & Galinsky, A. D. (2016). The too-much-precision effect: When and why precise anchors backfire with experts. *Psychological Science*. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797616666074

## Flat rates beat pay-per-use even at a premium

**Rule.** Offer an all-in flat rate priced above the expected pay-per-use total; keep pay-per-use as an entry option.
**Effect.** Purchasing professionals chose the flat rate even at a 20% and, less often, 50% premium over the pay-per-use equivalent; a $6,000 fixed fee was preferred to $50/h × 100 h.
**Why.** People overestimate their usage, want a spending cap, dislike watching a meter run, and see flat rates as harder to game.
**Source.** Kienzler, M., Kowalkowski, C., & Kindström, D. (2021). Purchasing professionals and the flat rate bias: Effects of price premiums, past usage, and relational ties on price plan choice. *Journal of Business Research*. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbusres.2021.04.024

## Simple price components raise willingness to pay

**Rule.** Round component prices, matching endings across components, no separate service fee, options easy to compare.
**Effect.** Customers were willing to pay more for the simpler price structure; complex structures were judged more expensive and less fair.
**Why.** Faced with complexity, people skip the calculation and assume the complex option costs more. If they do calculate, they resent the effort and suspect it was deliberate.
**Source.** Homburg, C., Totzek, D., & Krämer, M. (2013). How price complexity takes its toll: The neglected role of a simplicity bias and fairness in price evaluations. *Journal of Business Research*. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbusres.2013.05.049 · Related: *Journal of Marketing Research*. https://doi.org/10.1509/jmr.12.0438

## Show the price below the product

**Rule.** Place the price beneath the product image, not above it.
**Effect.** The same $2.49 dental floss price was judged 9% lower when shown below the product; a liquor store's sales were 35.2% higher with prices below the bottles.
**Why.** "Down = less, up = more" is a deep spatial metaphor; a lower position primes a lower magnitude.
**Source.** Barone, M., Coulter, K., & Li, X. (2020). The upside of down: Presenting a price in a low or high location influences how consumers evaluate it. *Journal of Retailing*. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jretai.2020.02.003

## Red prices look cheaper to men

**Rule.** Mostly male audience → all prices in red. Never mix red and black.
**Effect.** Men rated red prices as much better value than the same prices in black; women were unaffected. The effect vanishes under close scrutiny (expensive, important purchases).
**Why.** Red grabs male attention and is processed less analytically. Selective red flags the discounted items as manipulation, which backfires.
**Source.** Puccinelli, N. M., Chandrashekaran, R., Grewal, D., & Suri, R. (2013). Are men seduced by red? *Journal of Retailing*. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jretai.2013.01.002 · Mixed coloring backfires: *Psychology & Marketing* (2020). https://doi.org/10.1002/mar.21331
