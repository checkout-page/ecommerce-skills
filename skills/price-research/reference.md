# Evidence: price-research

Peer-reviewed findings behind the `price-research` procedure.

## The de-biased direct-question method

**Rule.** Ask half the sample an open "most you would pay" question and the other half a randomized "would you buy at $X?" question. Shift each open answer by the difference between the two survey means. The corrected answers give an individual-level willingness-to-pay distribution accurate enough for most pricing decisions.
**Effect.** In the validation studies the corrected estimates tracked real purchase behavior far more closely than either question alone, at a fraction of the cost of incentive-compatible methods.
**Why.** Open answers carry hypothetical bias (people overstate). Closed answers are unbiased on average but each respondent gives only one bit, and the price shown acts as a quality cue. The open survey supplies the shape of the distribution; the closed survey pins its level.
**Source.** Hofstetter, R., Miller, K. M., Krohmer, H., & Zhang, Z. J. (2021). A de-biased direct question approach to measuring consumers' willingness to pay. *International Journal of Research in Marketing*. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijresmar.2020.04.006

## Why the alternatives fall short

**Rule.** Don't average open answers; don't rely on competitor prices; treat Van Westendorp as a range finder, not an optimizer.
**Effect.** Open "how much would you pay?" answers have been found to overstate real willingness to pay by margins on the order of 50%. Competitor matching contains no information about your own demand curve.
**Why.** Hypothetical answers cost nothing; stated price ranges describe acceptability, not the point where (price − cost) × volume peaks.
**Source.** Same as above; the hypothetical-bias magnitude is discussed in Hofstetter et al. (2021) and the wider WTP-measurement literature it reviews.

## The incentive-compatible benchmark (BDM)

**Rule.** The Becker–DeGroot–Marschak auction is the accuracy benchmark; it requires selling the actual product under a lottery rule and is rarely practical.
**Effect.** Accurate individual willingness to pay, at high cost and complexity.
**Why.** Respondents have a real stake in their answer.
**Source.** Becker, G. M., DeGroot, M. H., & Marschak, J. (1964). Measuring utility by a single-response sequential method. *Behavioral Science*. https://doi.org/10.1002/bs.3830090304

## Survey design rules used in the procedure

**Rule.** 7 equally spaced prices spanning the plausible range; at least 30 closed answers per price; realistic product presentation; randomize both the survey assignment and the price shown; remove zero and implausible open answers before correcting.
**Effect.** With fewer than 30 per price the yes-share at each step is too noisy to anchor the correction; a 276-response run with 10 prices was reported as under-powered.
**Why.** The closed survey mean is estimated from per-price yes-shares; each share needs enough answers to be stable, and unequal steps distort the area under the curve.
**Source.** Practitioner rules derived from applying Hofstetter et al. (2021); the specific thresholds are not from the paper — treat as a practitioner tip.

## Choosing the price: profit maximum with a strategy override

**Rule.** Profit at price p = (p − unit cost) × audience × share of corrected WTP ≥ p. Take the maximum, then consider whether brand positioning (premium: go higher; reach: go lower) justifies a deliberate deviation.
**Effect.** Illustrative: 80% would buy at $50, 50% at $80, 20% at $140, audience 10,000, cost $20 → $240k, $300k, $240k profit; $80 maximizes profit, $140 may still be right for a luxury brand.
**Why.** Price also signals quality and selects the customer base you will have for future products.
**Source.** No primary citation on file; treat as a practitioner tip.
