# Evidence: product-visuals

Peer-reviewed findings behind the `product-visuals` procedure. Effects are as reported in the studies, in the context they were measured.

## Video, slideshows, or GIFs for hedonic products; stills for utilitarian

**Rule.** If the product is bought for pleasure or excitement, show it in a dynamic format. If bought for function, static images suffice.
**Effect.** People were willing to pay 79% more for hedonic products shown dynamically.
**Why.** Dynamic formats are more lifelike and make it easier to imagine using the product; imagining the experience shifts attention to feelings and away from price.
**Source.** Roggeveen, A. L., Grewal, D., Townsend, C., & Krishnan, R. (2015). The impact of dynamic presentation format on consumer preferences for hedonic products and services. *Journal of Marketing*. https://doi.org/10.1509/jm.13.0521

## Slow motion for premium hedonic products

**Rule.** Slow down action shots of the product in motion (a pour, an unfold). Works mainly for premium or luxury products bought for enjoyment.
**Effect.** Sales +22%, ad clicks +18%, willingness to pay +11%.
**Why.** Slow motion lengthens attention on the product and heightens the sense of quality and savoring.
**Source.** No primary citation on file; treat as a practitioner tip.

## Model gaze: away for hedonic, direct for informational or negative-emotion messages

**Rule.** For pleasure products, the model looks away from the camera. For feature/price information or negative-emotion appeals, the model looks at the camera.
**Effect.** In an A/B test, a sun hat sold 30% more and the ad was rated 19% better with the model looking away.
**Why.** An averted gaze lets viewers project themselves into the model's place; a direct gaze marks the model as someone else, which suits credibility and eye-contact for information.
**Source.** To, R. N., & Patrick, V. M. (2021). How the eyes connect to the heart: The influence of eye gaze direction on advertising effectiveness. *Journal of Consumer Research*. https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucaa063

## Contextual backgrounds for women's clothing

**Rule.** Show women's clothes worn in a fitting setting rather than on a plain white background. No effect for men's clothes.
**Effect.** Purchase likelihood was higher with contextual backgrounds for womenswear.
**Why.** Context helps the viewer imagine the occasion for the garment.
**Source.** Study on model backgrounds in online apparel presentation. *Journal of Business Research* (2021). https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbusres.2020.10.002

## Space products apart

**Rule.** Increase the empty space between products in layouts and multi-product images.
**Effect.** Liking and valuation of a product rose 11.4%; sales almost doubled.
**Why.** Separation makes each object easier to delineate and process, which reads as beauty and raises perceived value.
**Source.** Sevilla, J., & Townsend, C. (2016). The space-to-product ratio effect: How interstitial space influences product aesthetic appeal, store perceptions, and product preference. *Journal of Marketing Research*. https://doi.org/10.1509/jmr.13.0601

## Wide margins in ads; related products clustered in the center

**Rule.** In online ads, leave generous empty space at the edges; if several related products appear, group them near the center.
**Effect.** Clicks increased up to 3×. Not tested on websites.
**Why.** Empty margins direct attention to the grouped products.
**Source.** No primary citation on file; treat as a practitioner tip.

## Show multiple copies of a single-function product

**Rule.** If the product is bought for one specific function, show several copies together (a six-pack rather than one can).
**Effect.** The product seemed up to 10.7% more effective at that function.
**Why.** Quantity of the same item amplifies the perceived strength of its single benefit.
**Source.** No primary citation on file; treat as a practitioner tip.

## Make the product shine

**Rule.** Position lighting to create a reflection on the product, polish it, or add the reflection digitally. Most important when the design is simple and appearance matters.
**Effect.** Products were rated 23% more attractive and people were 32% more likely to buy them.
**Why.** Attraction to gloss is instinctive (linked to the appeal of clean water); shine also makes products look newer and cleaner.
**Source.** Sharma, N., & Kumar, A. (2022). Drawn to the gloss: Examining the effect of product reflection on product aesthetics. *Journal of Consumer Behaviour*. https://doi.org/10.1002/cb.2128

## Show a first-person hand touching the product

**Rule.** Include a hand (real or digital) touching, holding, or using the product, shot from the viewer's own perspective.
**Effect.** Across 15 experiments, people were 16% more likely to buy. In VR, willingness to pay rose 32.5%.
**Why.** Viewers partly interpret the hand as their own, which creates a sense of ownership that raises valuation.
**Source.** Luangrath, A. W., Peck, J., Hedgcock, W., & Xu, Y. (2022). Observing product touch: The vicarious haptic effect in digital marketing and virtual reality. *Journal of Marketing Research*. https://doi.org/10.1177/00222437211059540

## Place graspable products on the right

**Rule.** Position a product that can be held toward the right of the image, oriented for the right hand. If the product can't be held, place a graspable object near it.
**Effect.** Signups to a supermarket loyalty program were 3× higher when a vegetable peeler was positioned on the right of the billboard.
**Why.** Seeing an object positioned for the dominant hand (right for ~90% of people) creates a feeling of ownership that spills over to nearby items.
**Source.** Maille, V., Morrin, M., & Reynolds-McIlnay, R. (2019). On the other hand…: Enhancing promotional effectiveness with haptic cues. *Journal of Marketing Research*. https://doi.org/10.1177/0022243719878390

## Color saturation changes perceived size

**Rule.** High saturation makes the product look bigger; low saturation makes it look smaller. Use whichever suits the product.
**Effect.** Purchase likelihood rose when saturation matched the desired size impression.
**Why.** Saturation is read as a cue of physical presence and mass.
**Source.** Hagtvedt, H., & Brasel, S. A. (2017). Color saturation increases perceived product size. *Journal of Consumer Research*. https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucx039

## Surround the focal product with complementary products

**Rule.** Display accessories and add-ons around the main product, in a varied mix of shapes and colors.
**Effect.** Attention to and purchase of the main product increased; sales of the complementary items were unchanged.
**Why.** Complex displays are more engaging; complementary (not unrelated) items keep the added attention on the focal product.
**Source.** Radon, A., Brannon, D. C., & Reardon, J. (2021). Ketchup with your fries? Utilizing complementary product displays to transfer attention to a focal product. *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services*. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jretconser.2020.102339

## Future-oriented products right, past-oriented left

**Rule.** Place high-tech or future-oriented products on the right side of an image and vintage or past-oriented products on the left. Reverse for audiences reading right to left.
**Effect.** Liking was higher when placement matched temporal orientation.
**Why.** Reading direction maps time onto space: left is past, right is future.
**Source.** Chae, B., & Hoegg, J. (2013). The future looks "right": Effects of the horizontal location of advertising images on product attitude. *Journal of Consumer Research*. https://doi.org/10.1086/669476

## Shoppable and personal-feeling video

**Rule.** Social-media-style shoppable video on the product page, showing the product in action or a video review, with a personal, near-homemade feel at high quality.
**Effect.** Not measured in a controlled study.
**Why.** Extends the dynamic-format effect and lowers the distance between viewer and product.
**Source.** No primary citation on file; treat as a practitioner tip.

## Pretest creatives with polls

**Rule.** Where cheap, poll candidate images with the target audience before running an A/B test.
**Effect.** Faster iteration; no measured effect.
**Source.** No primary citation on file; treat as a practitioner tip.
